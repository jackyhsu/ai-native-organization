# The Founder Was Away for 9 Days. The Company Kept Learning

English | [中文](../zh-CN/stories/001-founder-away.md)

**Status: ✅ Worked—with governance limits**

**By Jacky Hsu**

On the ninth day of a trek in Kazakhstan, I could barely give the company one uninterrupted hour.

The hardest day involved close to nine hours of walking and roughly 800 meters between the lowest and highest points. By the second half, attention had narrowed to breathing, drinking water, and deciding where to put the next foot. I could glance at a few headlines during a break, but I could not seriously read a long article, review a regulation, or process a meeting record.

The world did not pause because the founder was in the mountains.

For a young company working with AI, a model update can change a product decision. A regulatory change can alter how data should be handled. A competitor announcement can reveal a new user expectation. “I will read it when I get back” sounds harmless, but nine days can be a long time when the environment changes daily.

Before the trip, we had assembled a rough company knowledge system. It was not a fully autonomous company brain. It was closer to an early sensing and preparation layer.

While I was away, the system continued collecting material relevant to the company, our products, and the AI industry. It kept the source, separated key facts from interpretation, and prepared a short note about why each item might matter. Material I saved during brief breaks could also enter the same queue.

By the end of the nine days, the system had generated nearly 200 candidate knowledge items.

That number felt magical for about a minute.

Then the harder question appeared: how many of those files deserved to become part of the company’s memory?

## Nearly 200 files were not nearly 200 answers

The generated items did not enter the formal knowledge base automatically. They waited in a `raw` area for review.

That boundary mattered more than the volume.

An article being summarized did not make the article correct. A recommendation being generated did not make the recommendation wise. If a summary removed an important limitation, mixed reporting with opinion, or connected the news to outdated company context, automation could make us wrong with greater confidence.

So the system could prepare a change, but it could not quietly promote that change into official memory.

When I returned to focused work, each candidate could be accepted, rejected, edited, or sent back for more evidence. Some became useful context. Some were connected with earlier observations. Some were simply discarded.

The company had continued to sense, read, organize, and propose. It had not made final decisions in my absence.

That distinction is easy to lose in conversations about autonomous agents. A system that produces a large number of documents may look active while creating a new information problem. More knowledge files do not automatically produce better judgment. More agents do not automatically produce a smarter organization.

## What changed for us

In many small companies, the founder becomes an extremely fast but non-redundant processor. Important news is forwarded to one person. Product context lives in that person’s memory. Feedback waits for that person’s interpretation. The arrangement feels efficient—until that person is unavailable.

Our nine-day test suggested a different possibility.

The company did not need the founder to remain online for every act of collection and preparation. It needed the founder and other responsible humans at the points where evidence becomes a decision, a rule, or an action the company is willing to own.

This is what we mean by **Human on the Loop**. Humans do not have to carry every piece of information through every step. They define intent and boundaries, review evidence, approve consequential changes, handle exceptions, and remain responsible for the result.

The experiment worked, but only because “candidate knowledge” and “company memory” were treated as different states.

Without that boundary, the same system could have made the company noisier rather than more intelligent.

## The open question

Nearly 200 candidates are manageable once. What happens when the system produces that amount repeatedly?

Human review can become the next queue. Old knowledge can remain after reality changes. Sources can conflict. A company brain can grow faster than the company’s ability to govern it.

Our next test is not simply whether the system can read more. It is whether it can help the team identify what deserves attention, preserve the source of every important claim, and allow uncertain material to remain uncertain until a human decides otherwise.

The founder was away. The company kept learning.

Now we have to learn how to stop the company from remembering the wrong things.
