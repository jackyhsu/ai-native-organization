# 000 The Founder Was Away. The Company Kept Growing

English | [中文](../zh-CN/stories/000-founder-away.md)

**By Jacky Hsu**

## The founder was away. The company kept growing.

By the ninth day, my body could no longer give the company one uninterrupted hour.

Yet during those nine days, the company had read nearly two hundred articles.

I was trekking in Kazakhstan, in Central Asia. On the hardest day, we walked for close to nine hours, with roughly 800 meters between the lowest and highest points. The trail kept climbing, then sending us back down. By the second half, my body had only the simplest instructions left: lift one foot, put it down; lift it again, put it down again. Breathing, drinking water, and deciding where to place the next step consumed all my attention.

When people approach their physical limit, it becomes difficult to pretend that willpower is infinite.

I could not seriously finish a long article, much less work through meeting records, regulatory updates, or contracts line by line. Even when I took out my phone during a short break, I had only enough attention to scan a few headlines. When I saw news that might matter to the company, I saved it and told myself: I will read it when I get back.

But “I will read it when I get back” is often one of the most dangerous sentences a founder can say.

AI changes by the day. A model update may alter a product design. A compliance policy may change how data should be handled. A competitor report may reveal a new need—or show that the original direction has already fallen behind. Meetings, customer feedback, partnership discussions, and contracts do not pause because the founder is in the mountains.

What founders truly fear is often not nine days with less work.

It is that the world may have moved far ahead during those nine days while the company failed to notice.

## When I could not read, the company began to read

Before I left, we had assembled a still-rough company knowledge system.

It continuously collected news and material related to the company, our products, and the AI industry. Topics I saved during breaks also entered the processing queue automatically. AI read the original source, preserved it, organized the main facts, and then used the strategies, product definitions, and earlier discussions already in the company knowledge base to add two or three lines of advice for the founding team:

- Why is this worth noticing?
- Which company judgment might it affect?
- Does it suggest a risk, an operational improvement, or a new product feature?

After nine days, the system had generated nearly two hundred files of this kind.

They did not go directly into the company’s formal knowledge base.

They were placed in a directory called `raw`.

The directory resembles a pull request waiting for review. Generated code does not automatically belong in the main branch. Summarized news does not automatically deserve to become company memory, and neither do the facts, judgments, or recommendations drawn from it. AI can prepare a change, but it cannot quietly turn “generated” into “correct.”

When I had time to sit down and concentrate again, the files waited for review one by one. I could accept, reject, or edit them, or request more evidence. Some entered the main knowledge base after verification. Some became operating knowledge. Others connected with similar user feedback from the past, gradually developing into product definitions or even the starting point for a PRD.

In other words, while I could not focus on work, the company did not make final decisions for me.

But it did not stop sensing, reading, organizing, and proposing.

For the first time, I felt something very concrete: a company could continue learning while its founder was away.

## The founder used to be the company’s central processor

Traditional startups often treat the founder as a fast but unreplicable central processor.

Important news goes to the founder. Customer feedback waits for the founder’s judgment. The founder explains product direction and confirms partnership details. The more a team trusts the founder’s experience, the more likely it is to send every piece of information to the same destination.

When the company is small, this arrangement looks highly efficient. There are no complicated processes or layers of approval. Find that person, and you get an answer.

But the arrangement hides a fragile assumption: that person must always be online.

When the founder is in a meeting, on a flight, sick, on vacation, or walking a trail that takes nine continuous hours, the company begins to queue. Information waits in chat. Judgment waits in email. Product decisions wait for meetings. Even if everyone has Copilot and local work becomes faster, the whole organization can still be blocked at the same point.

The first question for an AI-native company, then, is not: “How much work can AI do for us?”

It is: when a person steps away temporarily, can the organization continue to remember, understand, and prepare what comes next?

This is also the clearest dividing line between “using AI” and “redesigning the company.”

If AI only helps one person write email faster, make slides faster, or organize meeting notes faster, it remains a faster tool inside the old company. Roles, reporting lines, handoffs, and approvals stay the same. Information remains attached to individuals, and tasks still wait for someone to push them forward.

But if external signals can enter the system automatically; if the system can combine those signals with organizational memory to form recommendations; if human-reviewed recommendations can become formal knowledge, operating rules, or product definitions; and if later results can correct the next judgment, then AI is no longer just a tool.

It begins to become part of the company’s operating system.

## Two hundred articles were not the answer

Of course, automatically generating nearly two hundred articles is not something to celebrate.

If most of them have no value, they are merely information waste produced faster. If a summary omits an important limitation, it may help the company make mistakes with greater confidence. If the system cannot distinguish reported facts, an author’s opinion, an AI inference, and advice to the company, the so-called company brain is only a file warehouse that is harder to clean.

More knowledge does not necessarily produce better judgment.

More agents do not necessarily produce a smarter organization.

The truly difficult question is how to govern this content. How much material should enter each day? What must a human review? How often should the system rebuild indexes and relationships? How should old tags be retired? Who may access commercial secrets, customer information, and employee data? As recommendations from news move closer to products and code, where must the system stop and wait for approval?

That is why the `raw` directory exists.

It is not a temporary trash bin. It is an institutional boundary. On one side are candidate judgments that machines read, organize, and propose quickly. On the other is formal memory for which the company is willing to take responsibility.

AI-native does not mean removing people from the loop.

It means people no longer need to stand inside every step, carrying information from one place to another. They can stand over the loop and intervene where facts, risk, resources, and responsibility change. AI keeps the system running. People define intent, review evidence, approve changes, and stop the system when necessary.

## Can a company grow a brain of its own?

When I returned from Kazakhstan, I faced more than two hundred articles waiting for review.

I saw a different form of company: news could enter memory; memory could form recommendations; recommendations could become product concepts; user feedback could trace back to features and code; and test results could return to the knowledge base and change how the system behaved the next time.

This chain is still far from perfect. It misses important information, creates incorrect links, and makes mediocre or unreliable suggestions. As the number of files grows, retrieval, graphs, permissions, cost, and review load all become new bottlenecks. Any claim that a company can now “run fully automatically” underestimates the responsibility and risk inside a real organization.

But the system has already raised a question that is difficult to ignore:

If a company can continuously sense, form memory, recommend actions, and correct itself through results, why should we continue designing organizations for a world of scarce information, serial execution, and judgments that all depend on individual people?

Those nine days showed me that human energy has a very clear boundary.

The value of an AI-native organization is not that it asks people to break through that boundary and become super-managers who are always online, always reading, and always responding.

Quite the opposite.

Its purpose is to let the organization continue operating beyond human limits while leaving final direction, responsibility, and judgment in human hands.

The founder was away. The company kept growing.

The real challenge is teaching it where to grow, what it must not touch, and when it must stop and wait for a person to return.
